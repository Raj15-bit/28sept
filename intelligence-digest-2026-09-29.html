01

The price of money is now setting the price of everything else

Borrowing costs hit highs not seen in about 20 years, and India's share market is paying the bill while its factories keep growing.

In short
  • Lending money to the US government now pays the most since 2007, and that pulls money out of shares everywhere.
  • India's market has fallen for 7 weeks in a row and is the worst major market in the world this year.
  • Yet India's factories grew fast in August, so the fall is about the cost of money, not about a broken economy.

What changed: safe money started paying a lot

A bond is a loan you give to a government or a company. The yield is the yearly return that lender asks for. When the safest borrower in the world, the US government, has to pay more, every other borrower has to pay more too, and shares look less attractive next to a safe bond.

That is exactly what happened on 28 September. The US 10-year yield rose 0.10 percentage points in a day to its highest level in 19 years, and longer loans rose too.

The rise is not only a US story. The average yield on government bonds across the world went above 4% for the first time since 2007. Japan's 10-year yield also rose and the yen weakened again, so even the country that kept money cheapest for decades is now paying more.

Why the bond market moved so hard

There are 3 forces pushing at the same time. Oil is above $100 because of the war around the Strait of Hormuz, so prices keep rising. The US Federal Reserve (the Fed, America's central bank) raised its rate on 16 September instead of cutting it. And the US government keeps borrowing about $2 trillion more each year than it collects.

  • Oil — Brent crude traded near $106–107 a barrel before falling back late on 28 September on a report that Iran may stop enriching uranium.
  • The Fed — it raised its range to 3.75–4.00% on 16 September; markets now see about a 70% chance of another rise on 28 October.
  • Debt — US debt is about $40 trillion; the rise in yields since the Iran war began will in time add roughly $546 billion a year to interest bills.
  • Real rates — Goldman Sachs data show almost all of the 1-point rise in the 10-year yield this year is in the "real" yield (the part above expected inflation), not in inflation fears.

That last point matters. If yields were rising only because people feared inflation, a peace deal could bring them down fast. A rise in real yields means investors want more reward to lend, because many governments and AI companies are all asking for money at once. So even a quick end to the war may not bring cheap money back.

How it reached Indian shares

The simplest test investors use is to compare the earnings yield of shares (company profit divided by share price) with the bond yield. For the Sensex, the earnings yield is now 5.10%, just below the 5.17% paid by the US 10-year bond. The gap has turned negative for the first time in over a year.

In plain words, a foreign fund can now earn slightly more by lending to the US government than by owning Indian shares, with far less risk. The Chief Economic Adviser, V Anantha Nageswaran, said investors usually want 2 to 3 extra percentage points for Indian shares. That is why foreign funds keep selling.

  • 28 September — foreign investors sold ₹5,353 crore of Indian shares; Indian funds bought ₹5,189 crore.
  • Bets on a fall — foreign funds hold about 3.2 lakh index futures contracts betting on a fall; 88% of their positions are bets on a fall.
  • The index — the Nifty closed near 22,780, down about 1.6% on the day; the Sensex lost 1,124 points; about ₹7.8 lakh crore of market value was wiped out.
  • The streak — 7 weekly losses in a row, the longest since the Covid crash of 2020.

The part the index hides: the real economy is growing

It would be easy to read the fall as a sign that India's economy is weak. The official numbers say the opposite: industrial output grew 8% in August, and manufacturing grew 9%.

  • Factories — electrical equipment output up 30.9%, cars and other vehicles up 25.2%, capital goods (machines used to make other things) up 16.9%.
  • Investment — the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) expects private companies to spend ₹3.2 lakh crore on new plants in FY27, up 23%.
  • Profits — analysts at Motilal Oswal expect Nifty company profits to grow about 16% a year over FY26–28, and small companies about 27%.

So the fall is mostly about the price of money, not about falling profits. The Nifty's price-to-earnings ratio (how many years of profit you pay for a share) has dropped from 24.8 to 17.6. Section 06 looks at whether that makes shares cheap.

Who gains and who pays

Higher rates hurt anyone who borrows and help anyone who lends. Banks lose value on the bonds they hold, companies with big debts pay more interest, and the rupee weakens, which makes imported oil dearer. Exporters paid in dollars, such as software and pharma firms, gain a little from the weak rupee, which is why Dr Reddy's and Infosys were among the few shares that rose on 28 September.

The chain below shows how 1 war in the Gulf ends up in an Indian investor's portfolio.

STEP 1War near Hormuz keeps oil above $100 and diesel at records
STEP 2Prices stay high, so the Fed raises rates instead of cutting
STEP 3US 10-year yield hits 5.27%, the highest in 19 years
STEP 4Indian shares now earn less than a US bond, so foreign funds sell
STEP 5Nifty falls 7 weeks in a row even as factories grow 9%

The big idea to hold on to: for now the bond market, not company results, is deciding what Indian shares are worth. That will change only when oil and yields calm down, or when profits grow fast enough to beat a 5% safe return.

02

Hard limits: oil routes, diesel, power lines and missing parts

The physical things that cannot be fixed with money or a speech, and how long each one takes to fix.

In short
  • Oil is moving through Hormuz again, but ships are being hit, so every barrel carries a heavy risk cost.
  • Diesel is scarce because the world closed refineries, and no quick fix exists.
  • AI data centres need power that grids cannot deliver for 7 or more years, so companies are building their own power plants.

Hormuz: open, but dangerous

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea channel between Iran and Oman through which a large share of the world's oil passes. Iran's navy struck 19 ships there over 2 nights, yet oil still flows at about two-thirds of the pre-war level. TotalEnergies' chief, Patrick Pouyanne, put it at about 10 million barrels a day.

  • Saudi Arabia — restarted its East-West pipeline, which can carry 7 million barrels a day to the Red Sea port of Yanbu and so avoids Hormuz, and tankers are loading on both coasts.
  • US forces — 8 US Marines were hurt on 14 September when an Iranian missile hit a ship; the Pentagon did not announce it at the time.
  • Talks — President Trump rejected Iran's 7-day ceasefire offer; late on 28 September a report said Iran agreed to halt enrichment, but a US source said "the gaps are wide".

Why ships keep sailing even when they may be hit comes down to simple arithmetic. A tanker and its cargo are worth about $280 million together, so the owner prices the chance of losing it into every barrel carried, as the table below shows.

Diesel: the refinery gap nobody can close quickly

Diesel runs trucks, trains, ships and farm machines, so its price feeds into almost everything. Europe has closed about 30 refineries in 15 years, and the plants that are left cannot make enough. US retail diesel now costs about $6.5 a gallon.

  • US ban idea — Trump said he is "thinking very seriously" about banning diesel exports to cool US pump prices.
  • Goldman Sachs model — each week of a ban cuts US diesel by about $0.25 a gallon at first, then raises US petrol by about $0.30 a gallon once storage fills, because both come out of the same refinery run.
  • Russia — plans to keep its own diesel export ban through October.
  • Europe — TotalEnergies called a US ban "a bad idea"; Eni capped Italian diesel prices for 30 days.
  • India — Indian refiners export diesel to Europe, so a US ban would make Indian refining more valuable.

There is a second, slower problem underneath. About 80% of world oil now comes from fields that are past their peak, and only about 10% from fields still growing. Big oil companies are paying cash to shareholders instead of drilling more, so supply may stay tight for years even if the war ends.

Power for AI: the grid is the bottleneck

AI chips can be built in months, but connecting a new data centre to the power grid now takes more than 7 years in the US, up from about 2 years before. Engine maker INNIO said this is why tech giants now sign 15-year contracts for power plants built next to the data centre, called behind-the-meter power.

The chart shows AI power demand more than tripling in 2 years. Morgan Stanley estimates a US power gap of about 33 GW even after counting new gas turbines and fuel cells. So the question is not whether AI demand exists, but whether the power can be delivered on time.

  • Goldman Sachs — expects 25% of all data-centre power to come from on-site plants by 2030, up from almost 0 in 2025.

Indian suppliers sit inside this chain. Kirloskar Brothers now earns about a quarter of its US business from data-centre pump systems, and Marine Electricals won ₹250.78 crore of power-distribution orders from 3 data-centre firms. The limit on AI is creating orders for plain electrical equipment.

India has its own bottlenecks, and they sit in the parts that are hardest to make.

The solar gap shows the pattern clearly. India added 50.6 GW of module assembly in the first half of the year but only 9.7 GW of cell making, so cells are now most of its solar imports. The easy step, assembly, has been built; the hard step, making the cell and the wafer inside it, is still imported.

Batteries follow the same path. Planned Indian battery and cell investment is about ₹43,300 crore, and firms such as Altmin (a 100,000-tonne cathode plant in Telangana) and Epsilon are building materials. But China holds over 90% of cathode and anode making. So for the next few years, every Indian battery or solar plant still depends on Chinese inputs, and those inputs can be restricted, as China did with battery technology exports last October.

Copper and metals: companies would rather hold metal than cash

A US electrical equipment maker was quoted saying he would rather have copper in his warehouse than cash in the bank, because he fears cash may not buy copper later. In India, LG said copper cost 34% more this year, and steel 24% more. China's copper import premium is at its highest since 2022.

When buyers start hoarding a material, prices rise faster than demand alone would justify. So the price rises that Indian consumers are now seeing on fans, ACs and TVs (section 07) are the last step of a chain that starts with metal hoarding and freight delays.

03

Money and rates: the Fed is raising, bonds are falling, gold is slipping

What the rise in borrowing costs is doing to bonds, gold, the US consumer and India's banks.

In short
  • The Fed raised rates in September and may raise again in October, which almost never happens before a US election.
  • Long-term US bonds have lost about 60% of their price since 2020, the worst fall in 40 years.
  • In India, the RBI is pulling cash out of the system and markets expect rate rises, which weighs on banks.

The Fed turned from cutting to raising

Early in 2026 most investors expected rate cuts. Instead the Fed, now led by Kevin Warsh, raised its rate on 16 September. Fed governor Lisa Cook said on 28 September that the "recent rate hike" was meant to fight inflation that is too high, and that jobs can handle higher rates.

Some reports described the September move as a cut; the Fed's own statement and news reports of the day show it was a rise to 3.75–4.00%. The difference matters, because a central bank that is raising rates into an oil shock is signalling it will not rescue markets soon.

  • October odds — futures price about a 68–70% chance of another rise on 28 October.
  • History — UBS notes the Fed has never raised rates in October right before a US election since at least 1990.
  • Jobs — September payrolls are expected at only 45,000–60,000 against 162,000 in August; unemployment near 4.1%.
  • The Treasury — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent asked the Fed to keep an "open mind", arguing AI productivity will hold prices down.

The long bond has had its worst fall in 40 years

When yields rise, the price of an existing bond falls, because new bonds pay more. The line below shows what the US government now pays to borrow for different lengths of time. Loans of 20 and 30 years cost the most, which means lenders want extra pay to lock money away for decades.

The index that tracks the prices of the longest US government bonds has fallen about 60% since 2020, far worse than its fall in the 2008 crisis, the previous record. So anyone who bought these bonds as a safe asset has lost more than in any earlier crash.

  • Bond fear gauge — the MOVE index, which measures how much bond yields are jumping around, rose 19% in 1 week, its biggest weekly jump since April 2025.
  • Shares vs bonds — over 6 months US share futures beat 10-year bond futures by 23.3 percentage points, the widest gap since 2021.
  • A strange calm — share fear gauges such as the VIX stayed low, while bond and credit fear gauges were near their highest ever.
  • Cheaper borrowing via short bills — about $8 trillion of US debt must be rolled over within 1 year, so higher rates hit the budget fast.

This split between calm shares and panicking bonds is unusual. Either bonds are wrong and yields will fall back, or shares have not yet priced in what bonds are saying. Section 10 sets out both sides.

The US consumer is under strain, but the rich are not

The University of Michigan's consumer sentiment index fell to 48.1, the 2nd lowest reading in its history. The survey blamed fuel prices, inflation and mortgage rates.

The top 0.1% of households gained about 15,000 times more than the bottom half. People who own shares and houses are doing well; people who pay rent and buy fuel are not. That is why US spending holds up in total while mood surveys collapse.

  • Rent defaults — Freddie Mac's apartment-loan delinquency rate rose to 0.64%, above its 2011 peak; the 30-year mortgage rate is about 7.4%.
  • Factories — Texas manufacturers told the Dallas Fed that high interest and energy costs are "a double hit. We can't do any planning."
  • Retail investors — sold a record $300 million of share funds in 1 day, led by chip funds, and pulled $1.2 billion from a 3-times-leveraged chip fund in 1 week.

Gold fell hard, but central banks keep buying

Gold is usually a safe place in a crisis, but it pays no interest. When bonds pay over 5%, holding gold costs more. Gold fell over 3% on 28 September to below $4,200 an ounce.

Yet central banks are buying more than they report. Goldman Sachs estimates they bought about 44 tonnes in July, almost twice the 23 tonnes in official figures. So the long-term buyers of gold are still there; the short-term traders are leaving.

  • Silver — fell about 5% on 28 September, but refineries are backed up for 3–4 months in the US and about a year overseas, so physical metal stays scarce.
  • The bond-gold view — US long-bond futures have lost 93% of their value measured in gold since the euro launched in 1999.
  • Budget math — the US spends about $7 trillion a year but takes in $5.2 trillion, so letting inflation shrink the debt may be the easier path.

India: the RBI is draining cash, and banks feel it

The RBI sold ₹1 trillion of government bonds in September through open market operations (selling bonds to pull cash out of banks). Surplus cash in the banking system fell from a record ₹11.16 trillion to ₹4.31 trillion. Less spare cash means higher short-term rates.

Banks are hit from 3 sides. Rising yields cut the value of the bonds they hold, deposits cost more because some banks raised money through FCNR deposits (foreign-currency deposits from non-resident Indians) at high rates, and loan demand slows. Analysts at an Anand Rathi conference expect margin pressure in the July–September quarter and recovery later in the year.

Some lenders face a further problem. Many small-loan lenders have written off 10–15% of their past loan book beyond their loss provisions. Write-offs remove bad loans from the reported bad-loan ratio, so the ratio can look clean while losses are large; both numbers must be read together.

A simple comparison shows how Indian and US rates now line up. Amazon's dollar bond due in 2036 yields 6.05%, while state-owned Power Grid just borrowed for the same length in rupees at 7.74%. Since the rupee keeps losing value against the dollar, the returns end up close; Indian bonds pay more mainly to cover the weaker currency.

Trade: a thin US–China deal, a new US tariff threat for India, and who wins on medicines

Where the trade headlines point one way and the details point another, and who actually pays or collects.

In short
  • The US and China cut tariffs on a small list of goods, and markets were disappointed.
  • A new US law lets the President put tariffs of up to 100% on India over Russian oil, with a decision due by 18 October.
  • Indian medicine and chemical makers are winning orders as Europe's plants close and Novo Nordisk starts outsourcing.

The US–China deal was smaller than hoped

After Xi Jinping's state visit to Washington, the US and China agreed to cut tariffs to normal "most favoured nation" rates on about $30 billion of goods each way. They extended their trade truce to 10 January 2027 and set up a new Board of Trade to manage disputes.

The Chinese goods on the US list are mostly toys, fireworks, household items and sports gear, not strategic products. The average US tariff on Chinese imports stays near 23%. So the deal lowers tension without changing the basic split between the 2 economies.

  • Chip curbs — proposed US limits on Chinese optical parts for data centres hit Zhongji Innolight (−9%) and Eoptolink (−8%); China's ChiNext index fell 4.5%.
  • Arms — the US ambassador to China, David Perdue, said Trump asked Xi if China wanted to buy American weapons; a $14 billion arms package for Taiwan remains on hold.
  • Iran — Perdue said Xi repeated that China supports no nuclear weapon in Iran and promised to look into any Chinese help to Iran.

For India, the deal is a warning more than a threat. Trump went out of his way for Xi, which suggests the US will deal with China directly rather than rely on India as a counterweight. That reduces India's bargaining power in its own trade talks.

India faces a new 100% tariff threat

On 18 September, President Trump signed the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, often called the Graham Act after Senator Lindsey Graham. It lets the President put a tariff of up to 100% on goods from countries that buy Russian oil. He has 30 days to decide, which runs to 18 October.

  • Exposure — Russia supplied about 1.8 million barrels a day to India in September, about 45% of India's crude imports.
  • Current tariff — a 10% US tariff under Section 301 has applied since February 2026, after the Supreme Court struck down the earlier 18% "reciprocal" tariff.
  • Talks — Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal is due to meet US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer at the G20 trade meeting in Milwaukee.
  • Wrong date circulating — some posts warned of an 8 October deadline; the law's 30-day window actually ends on 18 October, and the President can waive the duty.

The earlier experience matters: at one point the US applied tariffs totalling 52% on Indian goods. With oil above $100, cutting Russian barrels would be very costly for India, so the risk is low in probability but large in impact.

Europe's energy crisis is sending chemical work to India

Ineos is shutting its acetyls plants in Hull, England, which make acetic acid, acetic anhydride and ethyl acetate. They were the last large plants of their kind in Europe, and Ineos blamed UK gas prices that are 12 times US prices and 8 times the cost of coal-based production in China.

Indian makers of the same products are running full. IOL Chemicals says its acetic anhydride and ethyl acetate plants run at 98–100% of capacity, and acetic anhydride revenue has more than doubled to ₹125 crore. When a whole region stops making a chemical, buyers look for a new source, and India is one of the few with spare low-cost capacity.

Medicines: outsourcing is spreading

Novo Nordisk, maker of the weight-loss drugs Ozempic and Wegovy, said it will now use outside contract makers for the peptide medicine itself, not just for packing. Macquarie notes that moving Wegovy from an injection to a pill needs about 73 times more active ingredient per patient, and Novo wants 10 times more pill users by 2030.

  • Divi's Laboratories — Macquarie's top Indian pick, because it makes its own raw materials and is building peptide capacity.
  • Global rivals — WuXi AppTec has the most scale; Samsung Biologics could enter through its purchase of PolyPeptide.
  • Dishman Carbogen Amcis — makes parts for antibody-drug conjugates (cancer drugs that attach a poison to an antibody); sales rose from ₹1,714 crore in FY17 to ₹2,932 crore in FY26.
  • Morepen Laboratories — filed its first US approval request (ANDA) for Sitagliptin, a diabetes tablet.
  • A caution — many contract manufacturers (CMOs, which only make to order) now call themselves CDMOs (which also develop drugs) to earn a higher valuation.

Panacea Biotec shows how long this kind of export business takes to build. Its formulation exports rose from ₹21 crore to about ₹230 crore over 9 years, helped by a profit-sharing deal with Apotex for the cancer drug paclitaxel in the US and Canada, and new approvals in the UK and Africa.

Steel and aluminium: prices firm at home

Indian hot-rolled steel now sells at a 3% premium to imported steel, helped by a safeguard duty (an extra tax on imports to protect local makers). Morgan Stanley expects steel shares to do well over 12 months. Many plants around Raipur have stopped making billets (semi-finished steel bars) because selling electricity pays more during the power shortage, and that pushes billet and bar prices up.

  • Winners — JSW Steel, Tata Steel, SAIL and Jindal Steel from higher prices; Shyam Metalics, Gallant Ispat and Sambhv Steel from firmer billet prices.
  • Vedanta — asked Odisha to speed up a ₹1 trillion aluminium plan; tribal villages again rejected bauxite mining in the Niyamgiri hills.

The common thread in this section: when energy is dear and trade is political, production moves to the places with cheap power and stable policy. India gains where it has both, as in chemicals and steel, and loses where others set the rules, as in US solar.

Insurance: the regulator says sellers are paid too much

IRDAI, the insurance regulator, published a draft that would cap commissions by product, policy length and sales channel. Its reason is in the numbers: from FY23 to FY25 the money paid to distributors of life insurance more than doubled, while new premiums grew only 27%.

The chart shows seller pay growing several times faster than premiums. So the regulator's view is that customers were paying for sales costs, not for cover. The proposed caps include 25–30% of the first-year premium on a term plan, with lower caps for health policies and renewals.

  • PB Fintech (Policybazaar) — lost about 30% in 1 day, roughly ₹28,000 crore of value; it said the rule came "without consultation with distributors".
  • Bernstein — kept a ₹2,310 target, but estimates a 40% cut in PB Fintech's take rate (its share of each premium) and 36% lower FY28 revenue.
  • JP Morgan — says insurers gain over time; order of preference SBI Life, HDFC Life, Max Financial, ICICI Lombard, ICICI Prudential, LIC; cuts are deepest in motor, credit life and retail health.
  • PB Fintech's counter — it brings at least half of the young new health customers; without them insurers' books age and claims rise; it may start its own insurer.

So the same rule is bad for the seller and good for the insurer. Banks that sell insurance for their own group companies (bancassurance) lose more than agents, which is why JP Morgan prefers insurers that rely less on banks.

UPI fees: small charge, big shift in who pays how

NPCI, which runs UPI, will charge merchants a fee of 0.4% on payments above ₹2,000 from 15 October, capped at ₹300 per payment. Customers still pay nothing, and small shops with low monthly takings are exempt.

Railways, telecom, insurance and fuel pay a flat ₹5 instead.

  • Vijay Sales — about 15% of its payments come by UPI, often ₹5,000–20,000; director Nilesh Gupta said "it was a zero cost to us so far".
  • Elara Capital estimates (FY27) — DMart ₹127 crore of fees (2.1% of operating profit), Nykaa ₹29.9 crore (2.6%), Trent ₹21.7 crore, Eternal ₹18.2 crore.
  • Card shift — shops are expected to push card discounts, cashback and EMI offers for big purchases; the average card payment is ₹4,413 against ₹566 on marketplace UPI.

The second-order effect is that credit card companies and card-issuing banks may win back big-ticket spending they lost to UPI. That matters because card spending growth has slowed sharply (section 07). Apple Pay is also preparing an India launch with Axis Bank credit cards, which charges the card, not UPI.

Korea's market is full of memory-chip makers such as SK Hynix and Samsung, and memory is the hottest part of the AI boom. China: build factories, not shoppers

  • Consumers — Chinese consumer shares are said to face a "lost decade" as Beijing focuses on AI; profits of Chinese industrial firms grew only 4.2%, the weakest since last November.
  • Housing — banks now offer 40-year mortgages, up from 30, and approval in 15 minutes, yet household loans fell by 1.03 trillion yuan in 8 months as people pay down debt.
  • Chips — memory maker CXMT plans 2 new projects worth 24.1 billion and 10.8 billion yuan.
  • Robots — a touch sensor for robot hands (1,140 per hand) fell from over 100,000 yuan when imported to 199 yuan made in Shenzhen, so humanoid robots now sell for under 100,000 yuan.
  • Batteries — Gotion and Volkswagen plan 3 joint ventures in Spain, Slovakia and Morocco, with Gotion investing about €1.6 billion; BYD is taking its fast charging to trucks with Foton.
  • Talent control — China now requires families of top AI and chip executives to get approval before travelling abroad, to stop know-how leaving.

China's approach is to put money into supply and technology, not into household spending. That creates cheap goods for the world, which is good for buyers but hard for competitors. India's solar and battery makers feel this directly.

Sodium batteries: China has already won the first round

Sodium-ion batteries use sodium, the element in table salt, instead of lithium. They store less energy per kilo (175 Wh per kg for CATL's best cell against about 205 for cheap lithium LFP cells), but they are cheaper, safer and work better in the cold.

Europe: expensive energy, shrinking industry

Europe is losing heavy industry to energy costs. Ineos is closing the last large acetyls plants in the UK, Europe has closed about 30 refineries in 15 years, and Italy's Eni is capping fuel prices. European Central Bank chief Christine Lagarde said wages have not yet responded to the energy shock, and traders see under a 40% chance of an October rate rise.

The political mood is changing too. French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said Europe must build its own military cooperation or become a "vassal" of China and the US. EU ministers are discussing a new plan to respond jointly to Russian sabotage and cyberattacks. Pope Leo XIV, on the other hand, warned against "the illusion of rearmament".

Are Indian shares cheap now?

The case for "cheap" is that the Nifty's forward P/E fell from 24.8 to 17.6 while profits are expected to grow about 16% a year. The case against is that the Sensex earnings yield is now below the yield on US government bonds, so shares still pay less than a safe bond.

  • For — 90% of Nifty shares are 10%+ below their highs, a level seen near past bottoms; Indian households keep buying through SIPs.
  • Against — small caps are still expensive (23.6 times profit); foreign funds hold record bets on a fall; oil above $100 hurts India more than most.
  • Middle view — small caps may drift sideways for weeks, with support near 17,500 and 17,100 on the Smallcap 250 index, until the US–Iran talks or July–September results break the range.
